Chemical Bonding for NEET: VSEPR Theory and Hybridization Explained

Published on October 23, 2026

Chemical bonding is one of the most fundamental concepts in NEET Chemistry, appearing consistently in multiple-choice questions and numerical problems. Understanding VSEPR theory (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion Theory) and hybridization are essential for scoring well in this section. These concepts not only help you predict molecular geometry but also enable you to understand molecular properties, reactivity, and bonding patterns—all critical for your NEET success.

This comprehensive guide will walk you through both VSEPR theory and hybridization, grounded in NCERT standards and aligned with NEET exam patterns. By the end, you'll have the clarity needed to tackle any bonding-related question with confidence.

Understanding VSEPR Theory and Molecular Geometry

VSEPR theory, introduced by Ronald Gillespie and Ronald Nyholm, is based on a simple principle: electron pairs (both bonding and non-bonding) around a central atom repel each other and arrange themselves in three-dimensional space to minimize repulsion. This arrangement determines the molecular geometry.

According to NCERT Class 11 Chemistry, Chapter 4 (Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure), the steps to determine molecular geometry using VSEPR theory are:

  1. Count the valence electrons around the central atom
  2. Determine the steric number (number of bonding pairs + lone pairs)
  3. Predict electron geometry (arrangement of all electron pairs)
  4. Determine molecular geometry (arrangement of only bonding pairs)

For example, in a water molecule (H₂O), oxygen has 6 valence electrons. With 2 bonding pairs (O-H bonds) and 2 lone pairs, the electron geometry is tetrahedral, but the molecular geometry is bent. This bent shape is crucial for understanding water's unique properties like high boiling point and polarity.

Common molecular geometries you must know for NEET include linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, and octahedral. Each has distinct bond angles that frequently appear in NEET questions. For instance, tetrahedral molecules have bond angles of approximately 109.5°, while trigonal planar molecules have 120°.

Key NEET Tip:

Always remember: lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs. This is why ammonia (NH₃) with one lone pair has a bond angle of 107°, not 109.5°. NEET frequently tests this concept through numerical and conceptual questions.

Hybridization: Blending Atomic Orbitals for Bonding

Hybridization is the process by which atomic orbitals mix to form new hybrid orbitals suitable for bonding. This concept bridges the gap between valence bond theory and observed molecular structures, making it essential for NEET Chemistry.

NCERT introduces hybridization as a theoretical framework to explain why carbon forms four equivalent bonds in methane (CH₄) despite having one s orbital and three p orbitals. The answer: sp³ hybridization, where one s orbital and three p orbitals combine to form four identical hybrid orbitals arranged tetrahedrally.

The five main types of hybridization you must master for NEET are:

  • sp hybridization: Linear geometry (180° bond angle) — Examples: BeCl₂, CO₂, HCN
  • sp² hybridization: Trigonal planar geometry (120° bond angle) — Examples: BF₃, ethene (C₂H₄)
  • sp³ hybridization: Tetrahedral geometry (109.5° bond angle) — Examples: CH₄, CCl₄
  • sp³d hybridization: Trigonal bipyramidal geometry — Examples: PCl₅
  • sp³d² hybridization: Octahedral geometry — Examples: SF₆, [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻

Determining hybridization is straightforward once you identify the steric number. The steric number equals the sum of sigma bonds and lone pairs around the central atom. A steric number of 4 indicates sp³ hybridization; 3 indicates sp²; 2 indicates sp; 5 indicates sp³d; and 6 indicates sp³d².

NEET frequently combines VSEPR theory with hybridization concepts. For instance, a question might ask: "What is the hybridization and geometry of sulfur in SF₆?" The answer requires you to count sulfur's 6 valence electrons, recognize 6 bonding pairs (steric number = 6), determine sp³d² hybridization, and identify octahedral geometry.

Common NEET Exam Patterns and Question Types

Based on previous NEET papers and NCERT standards, you'll encounter several recurring patterns related to bonding and molecular structure:

  • Direct identification questions: "The shape of PCl₅ molecule is..." or "The hybridization of carbon in C₂H₂ is..."
  • Bond angle comparisons: "Which has the maximum bond angle?" (comparing molecules like NH₃, H₂O, CH₄)
  • Property predictions: "Which molecule is polar?" or "Which has the longest bond length?"
  • Lewis structure correlation: Drawing Lewis structures and predicting geometry/hybridization
  • Exceptional cases: Molecules with expanded octets (like PCl₅) or incomplete octets (like BF₃)

A typical NEET question might state: "The shape of ClF₃ is...?" To solve this, identify chlorine's 7 valence electrons, recognize 3 bonding pairs with 2 lone pairs (steric number = 5), apply VSEPR theory to get trigonal bipyramidal electron geometry, and recognize that the molecular geometry (considering only bonding pairs) is T-shaped.

NEET Strategy:

Always draw the Lewis structure first. Count total valence electrons, distribute them around atoms following the octet rule, and only then apply VSEPR and hybridization concepts. This systematic approach prevents mistakes and saves time during the actual exam.

Practical Applications and Advanced Concepts

Beyond theoretical understanding, NEET expects you to apply these concepts to predict molecular properties. For instance, knowing that CO₂ is linear (sp hybridization) explains why it's nonpolar—the two equal dipole moments cancel out. Conversely, H₂O's bent geometry (sp³ hybridization) results in a net dipole moment, making it polar.

Understanding hybridization also illuminates reactivity patterns. Molecules with lone pairs (like ammonia) are nucleophilic, while those without (like boron trifluoride) are electrophilic. NEET often tests this connection between structure and chemical behavior.

Additionally, hypervalent compounds like SF₆ and PCl₅ challenge the simple octet rule. These molecules involve d-orbital participation in bonding (sp³d or sp³d² hybridization), demonstrating that the periodic table's structure groups elements with different bonding capabilities. NEET questions on these exceptions are common, so ensure you understand that expanded octets are valid in the third period and beyond.

Practice predicting structures for increasingly complex molecules: start with simple diatomic molecules, progress to triatomic species, then tackle polyatomic ions. Each step builds your intuition for electron arrangement and spatial geometry. This gradual progression, aligned with NCERT's Chapter 4, prepares you comprehensively for NEET's diverse question formats.
