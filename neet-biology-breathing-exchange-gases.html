Breathing and Exchange of Gases for NEET

Your complete guide to mastering the respiratory system chapter

Published on October 07, 2026

The chapter on "Breathing and Exchange of Gases" is one of the most critical topics in the NEET Biology syllabus. This chapter, typically covered in Class XI NCERT, encompasses the mechanics of respiration, the anatomy of the respiratory system, and the physiological processes involved in gas exchange. Understanding this chapter thoroughly is essential, as it accounts for approximately 8-12 marks in the NEET examination and frequently appears in both objective and short-answer questions.

Understanding the Respiratory System Anatomy

The respiratory system is a complex network of organs and tissues that work together to facilitate breathing and gas exchange. According to the NCERT Class XI Biology textbook, the system includes the nasal cavity, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, and lungs. Each component plays a vital role in the overall process of respiration.

The nasal cavity serves as the primary entry point for air, filtering, warming, and humidifying it before it reaches the lungs. The pharynx acts as a common pathway for both air and food, while the larynx (voice box) contains the vocal cords and helps protect the airway during swallowing. The trachea is a rigid tube that branches into two primary bronchi, each leading to a lung.

The lungs are the primary organs of respiration, divided into lobes (three on the right, two on the left). Within the lungs, bronchi progressively branch into smaller bronchioles and eventually terminate in tiny air sacs called alveoli. The alveoli are the functional units of the lungs where gas exchange occurs. Each alveolus is surrounded by a network of capillaries, creating the ideal environment for oxygen and carbon dioxide exchange.

💡 Key Tip for NEET Success

Remember that the alveolar surface area is approximately 70 square meters in the human lung—a crucial fact frequently tested in NEET. Draw labeled diagrams of the alveolar structure and practice labeling the roles of Type I and Type II alveolar cells during your preparation.

Mechanics of Breathing: Inspiration and Expiration

Breathing, or pulmonary ventilation, involves two primary phases: inspiration (inhalation) and expiration (exhalation). The NCERT curriculum emphasizes understanding the mechanical and muscular processes involved in both phases, making this a high-yield topic for NEET.

Inspiration is an active process where air moves into the lungs. The primary muscle involved is the diaphragm, which contracts and moves downward, increasing the volume of the thoracic cavity. Simultaneously, the external intercostal muscles contract, lifting the rib cage upward and outward. This dual action increases the intrapulmonary volume, decreasing the pressure inside the lungs below atmospheric pressure. Consequently, air flows in passively—a phenomenon explained by Boyle's Law of gases.

Expiration, during quiet breathing, is a passive process. The diaphragm relaxes and moves upward, while the external intercostal muscles relax, allowing the rib cage to descend. The elastic recoil of the lungs aids in expelling air. However, during forced expiration (such as during exercise), the internal intercostal muscles contract, further reducing the thoracic cavity volume and actively expelling air.

NEET questions frequently ask about the role of the diaphragm, respiratory volumes (tidal volume, inspiratory reserve volume, expiratory reserve volume, residual volume), and lung capacities. Make sure to memorize standard values: tidal volume is approximately 500 mL, vital capacity is about 3.5 liters, and total lung capacity is around 6 liters in adult males.

Mechanism of Gas Exchange and Transport

Gas exchange occurs through the principle of diffusion, where oxygen and carbon dioxide move across concentration gradients. In the lungs (pulmonary respiration), oxygen diffuses from the alveolar air (higher partial pressure) into the capillary blood (lower partial pressure), while carbon dioxide moves in the opposite direction.

Oxygen transport in blood occurs in three ways: approximately 97% binds to hemoglobin in red blood cells to form oxyhemoglobin, about 2% dissolves in blood plasma, and the remaining percentage binds to other proteins. Carbon dioxide transport is more complex: about 70% combines with hemoglobin to form carbaminohemoglobin, 20-23% is transported as bicarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻), and 7-10% dissolves in plasma.

At the tissue level (tissue respiration), the partial pressure gradients reverse. Oxygen diffuses from blood into cells where it's used in aerobic respiration, while carbon dioxide produced in cells diffuses into the blood. The Bohr effect—how increased CO₂, decreased pH, and increased temperature shift the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve to the right, promoting oxygen release—is frequently tested in NEET.

Exam-Specific Patterns and High-Yield Topics

Based on NEET analysis, certain topics within this chapter consistently appear in examinations. These include:

  • Respiratory volumes and capacities—questions often ask students to calculate vital capacity or identify which volume is being described.
  • Role of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles—multiple-choice questions asking about paralysis effects or muscle involvement during breathing.
  • Oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve—understanding factors affecting oxygen release and the significance of the Bohr effect.
  • Percentage composition of oxygen and carbon dioxide transport—students must recall exact percentages for each transport mechanism.
  • Partial pressures of gases in different regions—calculations involving PO₂ and PCO₂ in alveoli, blood, and tissues.
  • Regulation of respiration—chemoreceptors, baroreceptors, and neural control mechanisms affecting breathing rate.

Previous year NEET papers have included questions about conditions like emphysema, asthma, and fibrosis—diseases affecting gas exchange. Understanding how these conditions impair respiratory function can help answer application-based questions.
