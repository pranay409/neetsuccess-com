Neural Control and Coordination for NEET: The Nervous System Made Clear

Published on October 05, 2026

The nervous system is one of the most frequently tested topics in NEET Biology, appearing in multiple questions every year. From basic reflex arcs to complex neurotransmission, understanding neural control and coordination is essential for securing a high score. This comprehensive guide breaks down NCERT Chapter 21 (Neural Control and Coordination) into exam-focused concepts that will help you master this crucial topic.

Understanding the Nervous System Structure (NCERT 21.1)

The nervous system is divided into two main parts: the Central Nervous System (CNS) and the Peripheral Nervous System (PNS). The CNS comprises the brain and spinal cord, acting as the command center for all body functions. The PNS includes all neural tissues outside the CNS, connecting it to the rest of the body.

NEET exams frequently ask questions about the functional divisions of the PNS. Remember that the Somatic Nervous System controls voluntary movements, while the Autonomic Nervous System regulates involuntary functions like heart rate, digestion, and respiration. The Autonomic Nervous System further divides into the Sympathetic (fight-or-flight) and Parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) systems.

Key Points for NEET Success:

  • CNS acts as the integration and command center
  • PNS carries signals between CNS and body parts
  • Somatic vs. Autonomic distinction is crucial for 2-3 mark questions
  • Sympathetic activation increases heart rate; Parasympathetic decreases it

The Neuron and Nerve Impulse Transmission (NCERT 21.2-21.3)

A neuron is the structural and functional unit of the nervous system. Understanding neuron anatomy is fundamental—the cell body (soma) contains the nucleus, dendrites receive signals, and the axon transmits signals. NEET questions often test knowledge of different neuron types: sensory neurons carry signals to the CNS, motor neurons carry signals away from the CNS, and interneurons connect them.

The propagation of nerve impulses involves changes in membrane potential. The resting potential of a neuron is approximately -70 mV, maintained by the Na+/K+ pump. When a stimulus is applied, depolarization occurs, generating an action potential. This follows the All-or-None principle—the stimulus must reach the threshold to generate an action potential; otherwise, no action potential is generated.

NEET exams typically include 3-4 questions on action potentials, focusing on the role of voltage-gated ion channels, the sequence of events during depolarization and repolarization, and the concept of refractory periods. The absolute refractory period makes it impossible to generate another action potential, while the relative refractory period requires a stronger stimulus.

KEY TIP FOR NEET: Remember the mnemonic "DEPOLARIZATION = Na+ IN, REPOLARIZATION = K+ OUT." The inward flow of sodium ions during depolarization and the outward flow of potassium ions during repolarization are concepts tested in almost every NEET paper. Focus on understanding why these ion movements occur based on concentration gradients and electrical gradients.

Synaptic Transmission and Neurotransmitters (NCERT 21.4)

The synapse is the junction between two neurons or between a neuron and an effector cell. Synaptic transmission is the process of signal transmission from one neuron to another, occurring at the synaptic cleft. This region is incredibly important for NEET because multiple questions focus on the mechanism of neurotransmitter release and reception.

When an action potential reaches the axon terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels open, allowing Ca2+ ions to enter. This influx triggers synaptic vesicles containing neurotransmitters to fuse with the presynaptic membrane, releasing neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft. These neurotransmitters bind to specific receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, generating either excitatory or inhibitory signals.

NEET often tests knowledge of specific neurotransmitters and their functions. Acetylcholine is released at the neuromuscular junction and in the parasympathetic nervous system. Dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine are important in the brain and regulate mood and movement. GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid) is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain.

Common NEET Questions on Synapses:

  • Role of calcium ions in neurotransmitter release
  • Difference between presynaptic and postsynaptic neurons
  • Mechanism of receptor binding and signal transduction
  • Removal of neurotransmitters from the synaptic cleft (reuptake, enzymatic degradation)

Reflex Arc and Spinal Reflex (NCERT 21.5)

The reflex arc is one of the most important concepts in NEET Biology, representing the simplest neural pathway for a quick response to a stimulus. A typical reflex arc consists of five components: receptor, sensory neuron (afferent), spinal cord (nerve center), motor neuron (efferent), and effector.

The classic example tested in NEET is the withdrawal reflex—when you touch a hot object, sensory receptors in your skin detect heat and send signals through sensory neurons to the spinal cord. Interneurons in the spinal cord synapse with motor neurons that innervate muscles in your arm, causing you to withdraw your hand. Importantly, this reflex occurs without waiting for signals from the brain, demonstrating the efficiency of spinal reflex arcs.

NEET exams frequently ask about the advantages of reflex arcs: they are faster than conscious responses because the signal doesn't have to travel to the brain and back. They also help protect the body from immediate harm. Questions often require you to identify each component of a reflex arc or explain why a reflex is faster than a conscious response.

  • Receptor detects stimulus (sensory organ)
  • Sensory neuron carries signal to spinal cord
  • Synapse in spinal cord connects neurons (may involve interneurons)
  • Motor neuron carries signal to muscle
  • Effector (muscle) produces response
